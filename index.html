How to use it

  1. Shop with the calculator open. Enter 5–8 things you actually buy this week, then sort by cost per gram of protein. Chicken breast, eggs, canned tuna, Greek yogurt, milk and whey usually land at the top; cheese, protein bars and nuts at the bottom.
  2. Read “Cost for target” as a ceiling. It is what your whole day would cost on that one food. Real meals mix two or three of the cheapest, so your true daily cost sits near that number, not above it.
  3. Check protein kcal when you are cutting. A food can be cheap protein but carry a lot of fat or carbs with it — that column shows the trade.
  4. Prices stick in your browser only. Everything is stored in localStorage on this device. Nothing is uploaded, no account, no cookies from us.

Questions

What counts as “cheap protein”?

In 2026 US prices, anything under about 3¢ per gram of protein ($0.03/g) is cheap: eggs, chicken breast on sale, canned tuna, milk, dry lentils, whey bought in bulk. Above 6¢ per gram you are paying for convenience, a brand, or a bar-shaped package.

How much protein do I actually need?

Around 0.8 g per kg of bodyweight is the minimum for health; 1.6–2.2 g per kg supports muscle building when you train, and up to 2.4 g per kg helps keep muscle while cutting. The suggester above uses those ranges. More than that is not harmful for healthy kidneys, it is just expensive.

My label says “protein 24 g per 4 oz serving”. What do I type?

Switch the protein selector to /serving, type 24, and put the serving size in the serving-size box: 4 oz = 113 g. The calculator converts everything to cost per gram of protein, so mixing labels that use servings with ones that use 100 g works fine.

Does it handle pounds and ounces?

Yes — package size accepts lb, oz or g, and the price column can be shown per 100 g, per lb or per oz. The math uses 1 lb = 453.59 g and 1 oz = 28.35 g.

Where do the 26 presets come from?

They are realistic 2026 US retail prices and label values for common groceries — a starting point to compare against, not receipts. Overwrite them with your own prices; the value is the comparison, not our guesses.

Prices and nutrition values are the ones you enter. Results are estimates for planning, not medical or dietary advice. Protein needs vary with bodyweight, training and health — check with a doctor or dietitian if you are unsure.
